# Anatomy and foreshortening

## Proportion checks

- Compare head size with the ribcage. In the source lesson the first head came out too small and too far back.
- Place the knee lower than you expect. The elbow sits slightly above the waist line.
- The waist is wider in reality than the thin gap drawn between the two blocks.
- The ribcage is wider on a man and narrower on a woman. Women have rounder, wider hips and glutes. Men are narrower there. Abs are rarely visible on women.
- For a body measured in heads, make copies of the head down the body (an adult is about 8 heads in the notes, though one author stopped near 7.5 because 8 made the legs too long).

## Torso

1. Neck is a cylinder, ribcage is a large egg, pelvis is a box.
2. Clavicles form an S shape (seen from above) and run to the acromion on top of the deltoid. They are straighter on women because the shoulders are narrower.
3. The sternocleidomastoid runs from behind the ear to the pit of the neck. The trapezius sits on top and behind.
4. Pecs attach to the sternum and fan toward the arm under the deltoid, with accordion-like fibres. On a woman, draw the breasts as two circles over the pecs.
5. Abs: two thin pairs on top, then pairs 1 to 3 as paired blocks, and the lowest group as one big block. The navel sits about at the lower block.
6. Side: the external obliques are big masses. The serratus fingers interlock with the lats on the ribs.
7. Add ribs on the ribcage block and flesh on both sides of the belly.

- Everything in a pose hangs from the ribcage and the pelvis, so sketch these two first.

## Arm

1. Upper arm and forearm are two cylinders.
2. The deltoid is a teardrop on top of the humerus cylinder with front, middle and rear parts.
3. The biceps is in front and fairly boxy. The brachialis sits beside it. The triceps is behind (three heads).
4. The forearm has a flexor group on one side and an extensor group on the other. They wrap the cylinder and give a bump near the elbow. Two small thumb muscles shape the wrist-to-thumb contour. Straight tendon lines run to the back of the hand.
5. The clavicle and pecs attach toward the deltoid. One pec fibre goes up to the clavicle, which leaves a gap between deltoid and pec.
6. To memorise it, colour-code each muscle group on a new layer.

## Leg

1. Pelvis is a box, thighs and shins are cylinders, knee is a circle, foot is a ball plus a wedge.
2. Front thigh: the sartorius wraps diagonally from the hip down and inward and separates the adductors. The quadriceps is a teardrop on the inner side, a large mass in the middle and another on the outer side. There is a bump on the outside of the hip.
3. Shin: the tibia is a flat edge on the inner front. The calf (gastrocnemius) bulges behind. The leg profile runs in an S curve.
4. Back view: the glutes split in two around the sacral butterfly, the hamstrings are two masses, and the calf has two heads that end in the Achilles tendon above the heel.
5. Use opposing angles. Where one side has a corner, the other side has the opposite (straight against curve).

- The speaker admits that some muscle names are forgotten. Use the shapes, not the labels.

## Hands and fingers

1. Draw the back of the hand as a box and each finger as a tube. The thumb is a tube that starts low on the box.
2. Trace over with a bigger line and add knuckle bumps and the main tendon lines. Use several layers.
3. Bent fingers: reduce them to two tubes, with a rounded tip tube and a flat top. Use one block for the last two phalanges.
4. In a pose, draw the hand first, then fit the forearm and arm to it. Mark the mid-thigh position for a hand on the leg.
5. Photograph your own hand if you cannot copy it.

## Foot

- Rescue method: one big circle, a smaller flatter circle, an even flatter circle. Turn the stack in perspective and erase to the contour. Add the big toe as a sphere, then the other toes. From the front, draw two blocks. The top of the foot is rounded, not flat.

## Head and portrait planes

- Head from one ellipse: an ellipse for the cranium and a smaller ellipse or shape for the face and jaw. Draw contour lines through the head to set its direction.
- Mark the planes: temples at the sides, the brow ridge as a sunken shadow band, the jaw line on the flank, and the centre line following the head contour. Then add the features: nose as an ellipse, mouth as one line, eyes, brows.
- Neck is a cylinder. Ears are ellipses (ear height marks the head level in a tilted pose). Mark the hair zone last. In a pose, place the eyes, nose and mouth as dots first. The skull is wider seen from above.
- Portrait block-in: place the skull and jaw first. Mark the ear by diagonals toward the top of the brow and the base of the nose. Draw a mask shape around the eyes, then the mouth and nose. Add construction lines for cheek, zygomatic arch, mouth, nose and eyes.
- Study the Asaro head (a planes-of-the-head model) and the book "Anatomy for Sculptors". Light bounces differently on each plane, which explains the value choices. Do not copy pixel for pixel. Interpret.
- For stylized sculpt proportions: eyes at the centre of the head height, about one eye width between the eyes, ears between the eye top and the nose base and directly behind the jaw.

## Foreshortening

- Parts that come toward the viewer get bigger. The nearer side of the body is bigger, so check where the body faces.
- Exception: a limb that tapers toward its joints (hip and thigh large, smaller toward the knee) can get smaller as it comes forward.
- A head seen from below or at an angle is a box, not a circle. The neck is a short column. The jaw is a wedge below the head.
- Give the torso a side plane and 3D cross-contour guide lines. Draw head construction from a photo as an oval with a centre line and an eye line, with the hair left out.
- Long forms toward the viewer shorten, so use foreshortening with care and draw through the forms.
- Practise by dropping a cube along an arc in perspective and rotating cubes and cylinders. Without a sense of volume, a turning object fails.

## Shadow pass to check volume

1. Pick one light direction.
2. Place cast shadows (head onto the chest, arm onto the torso) and core shadows on the side away from the light. Wrap them around cylinders and around the navel and pec forms.
3. You can fill a far leg in the back entirely dark.

- Logic matters more than polish. Do this after the line pass. It helps you decide why a muscle sits where it does.

## Review checklist for a figure

Use this list when you check a figure or character sheet for errors.

- Line of action: does one flowing curve run through the pose?
- Ribcage and pelvis: are they tilted against each other, and is the ribcage about the right size against the head?
- Joints: do the elbows, knees and wrists sit at plausible heights?
- Limbs: are lengths equal on both sides unless foreshortening explains the difference?
- Foreshortening: is the nearer part bigger, with the tapered-limb exception?
- Hands and feet: are they built from boxes, tubes and wedges, with the right size for the body?
- Silhouette: do any tangents merge outlines?
- Light: do cast and core shadows follow one direction?
