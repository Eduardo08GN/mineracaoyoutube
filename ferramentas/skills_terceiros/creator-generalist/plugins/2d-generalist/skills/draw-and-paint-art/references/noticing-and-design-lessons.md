# Noticing, composition and design lessons

Habits that lift any piece (a painting, a render, a background plate) from flat to finished. They apply to anime, painterly and realistic work.
Every entry comes from one source series. Blender-specific build steps are in `paint-stylized-backgrounds` `references/blender-card-backgrounds.md`.

## Plan light and silhouette first

Lighting plus the structure and silhouette of objects is about 90% of environment art and is hard to change later. Colour scheme and texture can be added later. Tighten and contrast the lighting, cast shadows down from roofs and windows where an area lacks definition, add colour variety (a yellow house wall), and work on the sky. Improvement came from design, not new software features. Avoid the technical trap: tools over composition. The same shader with bad value placement looks ugly, and the good look comes from where light and dark sit on each zone.

Indoors, ambient occlusion matters more because the sun is not direct. Outdoors, keep light from blasting into the viewer's eye.

## Art of noticing

Look at the specific subject in the specific light. A black cat in warm light has a rim light from the sun and harsher light on that side, dimmer highlights (black fur), bounce light on the unlit side, and translucent ears with an orange gradient where lit. Observe small things and combine tool skill with observation. Quality comes from checking value, colour and design against attractive references while you work.

## Detail comparison

1. Find an image like your scene that is better than yours.
2. List what it has (for example tiles with leaves growing through cracks, wood chips everywhere, a grungy feel).
3. Add those items to your piece.

## Value check in grayscale

Make a grayscale copy of your piece and of the reference. The reason yours is flat shows at once (in one case the floor was too dark and was brightened a lot). Then:

- Cast foliage or object shadows over bland areas.
- Add a brightness gradient from the light source: bright, less bright, dark. Add god rays.
- Use a Fresnel-style rim that is brighter where light hits, and a second one on the other side as bounce light on the unlit side.
- Raise highlight values over time and push light and shadow contrast.

## Ugly-phase checklist

Expect a piece to look good only at about 70% done, and expect it to look worse right after lights go in. Do not quit. Ask:

- Is there a clear focal point?
- Is the lighting good?
- Is there enough detail?
- Does too much detail somewhere steal the focal point?
- How does it compare side by side with the inspiration?

Take a break of a few days before the final look. When your eyes tire, change the viewport or canvas background brightness to see it fresh.

## Final attack checklist

Run this when the piece is nearly done.

1. Add foreground elements and fill corners to frame the image (a chair, a leaf vine, a rose).
2. Add complementary colours (red flowers against green).
3. Detail flat or repetitive areas (boxes, a varied floor).
4. Test lighting strength: darker shadows, brighter highlights, a warmer (orange) tint.
5. Take risks, and undo what fails.

## Composition and focus

- Make big shapes first. Do not polish a small pot that barely shows.
- Every object must read two shades at least (a light and a shadow). Source: same video.
- Put an interesting living subject in the centre. Light the subject and foreground more than the background.
- Check composition in grayscale: rocks vary in shape, size and rotation, sun shine pulls the eye to one area, and quieter grass and leaves elsewhere give the eye rest.
- Keep focal elements detailed and give off-camera items little care. Imperfection looks real.

## Colour and variety

- Shift hue or saturation across parts, and keep values harmonious. Painterly style shifts hue and saturation near cloth edges and uses grunge. Anime stays sharp and bold.
- Break up colour: blue splashes on brown wood, pink strokes on leaves and mushrooms.
- Warm and cool contrast: an orange gradient where light hits, blue elsewhere.
- Keep a low-key background value for a lit subject (about 0.19 in one scene).

## Five ingredients of beautiful stylized art

1. Reference merging: merge qualities of several beautiful references. View them far away or squint to see layout.
2. Light and colour contrast: complementary colours (red and green) and natural palettes (blue, white, green). Enough, not deep fried.
3. Gradients: a stepped gradient gives a contrasty anime look. A linear gradient can look plasticky.
4. Enough detail, not photoreal: use a natural pattern as the detail (for example koi red and white).
5. Variety: different sizes, rotations and speeds.

## Tips that apply to characters and props

- There are more than two lighting levels. Add an environmental top light with extra shadow areas, drawn in or as ambient occlusion.
- Contrast bright and dark details: a scratch is dark then bright under it, a fold the opposite. Use it sparingly for anime.
- Judge fashion and shape by putting references on a board, taking a screenshot, flipping it and zooming far out.
- Let it cook: stop, return later, compare to references, and view on a phone at low and full brightness.
- Do not compare apples and oranges. Take inspiration from images that match your stage (neutral lighting for faces).
- Keep face forms crisp (sharp under-eye, nose, lips) and push darkness in.
- Accessories help up to a point, then give detail overload. Test each one and remove it if it overloads. Add asymmetry and silhouette interest.
- Design hair with big strands that define the form and small strands that complement them, with overlapping shapes that cast deliberate shadow shapes. Check it from several angles.

## Reference habits

- Collect a board per component (hair, face, upper clothing, lower clothing) and take one standout detail per image, not one whole sheet.
- Place references beside the work. Use real faces with similar makeup for a portrait. Study the film you imitate (water in Spirited Away). Synthesize references into something new.
- Take light from above while painting so highlights sit on top edges and shadows below.
- For motion, film yourself doing the action, then exaggerate.

## Process habits

- Start the piece you want to make. An hour-long prototype shows what to look up.
- Add one new element each project so skills grow together.
- Test short, emotional pieces before a series, see what gets traction, and write down why a failure failed.
- Screenshot your work over time.
- Keep it simple: base colours and texture first, lighting last.
