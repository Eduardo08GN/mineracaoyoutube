## Line and curve control

- Draw big lines with the whole arm or forearm and small lines with the wrist. Place dots on the page and connect them in one stroke at different angles. Rotate the canvas like turning paper. Avoid wobbly lines.
- Practise curves freehand, not with the line tool. Curves give rhythm: spine, ribcage and head as a curve, a leg with an opposing curve for balance, grass, hair.
- The aim is to approximate shapes freely, not to trace.

## Flow first (line of action)

- Draw one long S-curve line from the neck down the spine before any shape. Then add a tilted ribcage box, a pelvis box and limb lines that follow the curve. If there is no flow, the pose is hard to draw.
- Carve the shape one cut at a time, as simple as possible. Add detail along the curves, then clothes wrinkles. Draw a dark clean outline only when the shape is right.

## Two-block torso and reusable limb figure

- Ribcage is a trapezoid, wider at the top. The pelvis is a smaller block with a curved bottom edge. Leave a narrow gap between them. Draw the gap thinner than the real waist on purpose.
- Add a collarbone bar on top of the trapezoid and a shoulder circle at each top corner.
- Draw each limb as a short thick upper segment and a longer tapered lower segment, with a circle at every joint. Use the same figure for arm and leg. The elbow falls slightly above the waist line. Draw the knee as a short straight line across the joint.
- The legs: flow lines first, joint circles, thigh as a diamond with rounded ends. The lower leg reuses the arm figure. The calf line sticks out and goes in at the ankle.

## Front view then three-quarter view

- Front view: a vertical centre line and joint circles.
- Three-quarter view: also draw the side planes of the torso (two shapes plus a triangle on each side). The hidden side is a simple outline. The outer silhouette line is what changes between the two views and is the most important line. Add flesh on both sides of the belly and ribs on the ribcage block.

## Forms: sphere, cube, cylinder, cone

- Sphere: a circle plus a cross for direction, or an ellipse inside. A silhouette alone does not read as 3D.
- Cube: it carries x, y and z. Draw a plane, copy it below and join the corners. Draw through hidden edges. Learn 1, 2 and 3-point perspective. Cubes are the most important form.
- Cylinder: two circles (the same ellipse twice) joined by a tube. Wrap contour lines around it. Use cylinders for limbs and wheels, with spheres for joints. Contour lines get finer and closer in the distance, so a cylinder pointing at the viewer reads as such.
- Cone: a circle base extended.
- Combine them. Head: a sphere plus a boxy jaw, or one box with a circle inside to place the cranium. Hand: box palm, cylinder fingers, boxy wrist, wedge thumb. Foot: heel ball, mid ball and a flat ball joined, toes as ellipses and an arch on top, or a wedge with a box behind.
- Draw freehand so the method works from imagination.
- Practise circles and ellipses in space (good enough to build with), then boxes in perspective. Use them for limbs, torso, pelvis, knee, shoulder and hand.

## Shape language

- Practise circles, rectangles and triangles, squashed and stretched (a droplet is still a circle).
- Circle means cute: shrink the facial features and bring the eyes closer to push it further. Square means solid and strong: make the eyes squarish too. Triangle means aggressive: horns, pointed chin, teeth.
- Exercise: take one round, one boxy and one triangular random blob and make a head from each. Examples: Mike (sphere), Sulley (box), Maleficent (triangles).
- Shape also drives silhouette and colour. This works in any style. A realistic character uses the same shapes with softer edges.

## Pose study from 3D reference

1. Use a 3D pose viewer such as Posemaniacs (free). Pick a pose with a strong silhouette and negative space.
2. Start with the ribcage and pelvis and their tilt, not the head. This gives contrapposto, with shoulders and hips tilted against each other.
3. Add the spine and neck, then the legs from big shapes. Draw through the forms. Use foreshortening with care.
4. Place the head, the face dots, hands as sticks and mitts, and feet.
5. Lower the sketch opacity (or erase lightly) and trace again. Repeat two or three times so only the important lines remain.
6. Do a straight-and-curve pass. Opposite a curve on one side, put a straight or broken line on the other.
7. Add the muscles you know, then the last line pass with varied weight.
8. Hide the sketch to check that the pose still reads.

- Draw 1 to 1.5 hours a day in a sketchbook. Put the reference on a second monitor, not on the canvas, so you analyse and redraw.
- In any drawing app, turn off "scale brush with zoom" so the line stays steady.

## Line weight, tangents and silhouette

- Where two shapes overlap, draw the front shape's outline thicker and the rear shape's line thinner. The eye reads which is in front.
- Ink on a new layer above the construction (paper: blue pencil, then black pencil).
- Avoid tangents. Do not let an outline just touch another outline (a hand edge touching a hip edge). Overlap them or separate them clearly.
- Check the silhouette and the negative space between limbs and torso.

## Style notes

- Cartoon and anime: push the line of action, shrink the hands to simple forms, round the volumes. Three-fingered cartoon hands exist because hands are hard.
- Realistic: keep proportions close to the reference and soften the construction under the anatomy. Allow about 20 percent proportion change with Liquify early, then stop once the proportions are settled.
- Painterly: drop the clean line pass and paint values over the construction.
- Retro and low-poly: simplify to the big shapes and keep the line of action.
